\small\textbf{Abstract}: Device sizing for a high-speed receiver equalizer is a
constrained search over continuous device values in which every specification is
coupled through the same transistor dimensions, and in which the governing
figure of merit, the eye opening after a lossy channel, is observable only from a
transient simulation. Exhaustive parameter sweeps scale exponentially with the
number of sized devices and are therefore impractical at the resolution required
for a converged design. This paper presents AutoAnalog-RL, a sizing framework in
which a Soft Actor-Critic agent interacts directly with ngspice and learns a
sizing policy from simulated outcomes alone. The design target is a
source-degenerated continuous-time linear equalizer with a one-tap
decision-feedback stage for a PCIe Gen 2 (\SI{5}{\giga\bit\per\second}) receiver,
implemented in the IHP SG13G2 \SI{130}{\nano\meter} open process design kit using
foundry PSP103 compact models. Each environment step executes a fail-fast
sequence of DC, AC, transient-eye and linearity gates, so that infeasible
candidates are rejected at the earliest analysis that can reject them. The
trained policy reaches a design satisfying every target specification, across all
45 process--voltage--temperature corners, in a median of 2.53 simulations from an
arbitrary starting point, against 9 for CMA-ES and 13 for random search; measured
against an exhaustive sweep at practical grid resolution, this is a reduction in
sizing time of roughly four orders of magnitude per design instance. A two-stage
corner-randomised curriculum yields a policy that remains feasible at
corners withheld during nominal training (24/24 rollouts), a natural-language
interface maps engineering intent onto reward weights, and each run emits the
sized netlist as its deliverable. The optimisation additionally surfaced two
latent defects in the measurement pipeline that manual sweeps had not exposed;
both are analysed, corrected and covered by regression tests, and are reported
here as a methodological result.

\section{Introduction}

Serial links at PCIe Gen 2 rates and above operate over channels whose insertion
loss rises steeply with frequency. At the \SI{2.5}{\giga\hertz} Nyquist frequency
of a \SI{5}{\giga\bit\per\second} NRZ link, a representative FR-4 trace
attenuates by roughly \SI{10}{\decibel}, and the resulting inter-symbol
interference closes the received eye entirely. Receiver-side equalization
restores it: a continuous-time linear equalizer (CTLE) applies a
high-frequency boost that inverts the channel's loss slope, and a
decision-feedback equalizer (DFE) cancels residual post-cursor interference
without amplifying noise \cite{saif2021,shibasaki2015}.

Sizing such a front end is the difficulty. The five device values of a
source-degenerated CTLE(nput width, load resistance, tail current,
degeneration resistance and degeneration capacitance) set gain, bandwidth,
power, linearity and noise jointly, so no specification can be closed in
isolation. The specification that ultimately governs link operation, the vertical
and horizontal eye opening after the channel, is not visible in a small-signal
analysis at all; it requires a pseudo-random bit sequence transient through an
explicit channel model \cite{naidu2022}. An exhaustive sweep over five continuous
parameters is exponential in the grid resolution: even a coarse ten-point grid is
$10^5$ simulations, which at the throughput achieved here is roughly
\SI{17}{\hour} of wall-clock time for a single design instance, and a
twenty-point grid is over three weeks. General-purpose optimisers reduce this
count but must repeat the entire search whenever the target specification, the
process corner or the relative weighting of objectives changes.

Reinforcement learning addresses precisely this repetition. A policy trained once
over a distribution of starting points and operating conditions amortises its
cost: sizing a new instance becomes a short sequence of policy evaluations rather
than a fresh search. This idea has been advanced for analog sizing generally, and
system-level surveys of machine-learning-assisted SerDes design identify
simulation-in-the-loop optimisation of the receiver front end as a principal
application \cite{song2019}. The framework presented here realises it for a
complete PCIe Gen 2 equalizer on foundry-grade device models.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{A closed simulation-in-the-loop sizing framework.} A Soft
Actor-Critic agent drives ngspice directly over a bounded five-parameter action
space, with no surrogate model and no human intervention between the target
specification and the sized netlist. Candidate evaluation is organised as a
fail-fast gate cascade (DC $\rightarrow$ AC $\rightarrow$ transient eye
$\rightarrow$ linearity), so an infeasible design is rejected at the cheapest
analysis capable of rejecting it (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).

\item \textbf{Sizing on foundry compact models, not idealised devices.} All
reported results use the IHP SG13G2 PSP103 surface-potential models, compiled
from Verilog-A to OSDI and executed natively in ngspice
(Section~\ref{sec:backend}). A thread-pinning correction to the simulator
configuration yields a $65\times$ throughput improvement under parallel
execution, which is what makes training on a laptop-class machine tractable.

\item \textbf{Convergence in a median of 2.53 simulations, quantified against
baselines.} Deterministic rollouts reach a fully feasible design in 2--3
simulations across 144 rollouts, against 9 for CMA-ES and 13 for random search
from equivalent starting points, and against an estimated $10^5$ evaluations for
an exhaustive sweep at comparable resolution (Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\item \textbf{Reward shaping that produces margin rather than marginal
compliance.} Terminating an episode at the first feasible design leaves the
policy on the constraint boundary. Holding the episode open and paying an
explicit margin bonus moves the median eye height from \SI{0.43}{\volt} to
\SI{0.92}{\volt} against a \SI{0.5}{\volt} target
(Section~\ref{sec:reward-design}).

\item \textbf{Corner robustness by domain randomisation.} A second training stage
in which each episode is simulated at a corner drawn uniformly from the
45-corner matrix, with the corner identity withheld from the observation, yields
a policy that reaches feasibility at randomly drawn corners in 24 of 24 rollouts
(Section~\ref{sec:curriculum}).

\item \textbf{Specification retargeting without redesign.} The Nyquist frequency
is a framework input that propagates to the measurement frequency, data rate,
unit interval, transient step and channel pole. Resuming the trained policy at
\SI{1.25}{\giga\hertz} recovers full compliance within 3k steps, demonstrating
the tunability required of the peaking stage (Section~\ref{sec:tunable}).

\item \textbf{Measurement validation as a by-product of optimisation.} Two
defects in the measurement pipeline---spectral leakage in the HD3 estimator and a
decision-labelled DFE eye---were surfaced by the optimisation reaching regions of
the design space that manual sweeps had not visited. Both are analysed,
corrected, and covered by regression tests (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Circuit topology}

The equalizer is a differential NMOS pair with resistive loads and an RC
source-degeneration network (Figure~\ref{fig:ctle}), followed by a one-tap
decision-feedback stage. Degeneration introduces a zero at
$1/(2\pi R_s C_s)$ and a pole at higher frequency, so the stage attenuates
low-frequency content relative to its mid-band gain and produces the
high-frequency boost that inverts the channel response. Five values are sized:
input device width $W_\mathrm{in}$, load resistance $R_\mathrm{load}$, tail
current $I_\mathrm{bias}$, degeneration resistance $R_s$ and degeneration
capacitance $C_s$. All devices are IHP \code{sg13\_lv\_nmos} at the minimum
drawn length $L=\SI{130}{\nano\meter}$; the bounds in Table~\ref{tab:bounds}
define the action space.

\subsection{Channel and decision-feedback model}
\label{sec:dfe}

The transient gate drives a 127-bit PRBS7 sequence ($x^7+x^6+1$) at
\SI{5}{\giga\bit\per\second}, repeated twice so that the measured period is free
of start-up transients, through a two-section RC channel. Each section
contributes a real pole at \SI{1.7}{\giga\hertz}, giving approximately
\SI{-10}{\decibel} of loss at the \SI{2.5}{\giga\hertz} Nyquist frequency and
\SI{-20}{\decibel} at \SI{5}{\giga\hertz}---the loss slope characteristic of a
PCIe Gen 2 class FR-4 trace. The DC and AC gates bypass the channel, so the
reported peaking figure characterises the CTLE alone and remains directly
comparable to the specification.

The decision-feedback stage (Figure~\ref{fig:dfe}) operates on the unit-interval
centre samples of the equalized waveform and implements
\begin{equation}
y[n] = x[n] - b_1\,\hat{d}[n-1],
\label{eq:dfe}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{d}[n-1]$ is the slicer's own previous decision, so that error
propagation is represented rather than assumed away. The tap $b_1$ is either
swept over its bounds during validation or, in the whole-equalizer configuration
of Section~\ref{sec:equalizer}, sized by the policy as a sixth action.

\section{Sizing Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the closed loop. The policy emits a normalised design
vector; the evaluator maps it onto device values, renders a parameterised
netlist, and invokes the analysis gates in ascending order of cost. The resulting
metrics are reduced to a scalar reward and returned, together with the design
vector and the normalised violation of each constraint, as the next observation.
Only the simulator adapter is technology-specific: the specification, reward and
environment layers operate on metric dictionaries and are independent of both the
simulator and the process.

\subsection{Fail-fast evaluation}

The four analyses differ in cost by more than an order of magnitude: an operating
point resolves in milliseconds, an AC sweep in tens of milliseconds, and a
two-period PRBS transient on PSP103 models in approximately \SI{1.5}{\second}.
Executing them unconditionally would spend the majority of the simulation budget
characterising designs that a bias check already disqualifies. The evaluator
therefore terminates at the first failure, and the linearity gate is deferred
further still: HD3 is measured only once every other constraint is satisfied, so
it costs one additional simulation per otherwise-feasible candidate rather than
one per candidate. A design that fails to establish a valid operating point is
rejected in milliseconds and never reaches the transient gate.

\subsection{Simulation backend}
\label{sec:backend}

The SG13G2 transistor deck uses PSP103, a foundry-grade surface-potential compact
model, which ngspice loads as OSDI shared objects compiled from the kit's
Verilog-A sources with OpenVAF. Selecting this kit over the alternative open
\SI{130}{\nano\meter} process was a throughput decision: the loop executes three
to four analyses per environment step and tens of thousands of steps per training
run, and the PSP103/OSDI path sustains approximately \SI{0.6}{\second} per
environment step with eight simulators in parallel. Because PSP103 is the
foundry's own compact model, the choice strengthens the device physics underlying
every reported result rather than simplifying it. The framework retains a generic
Level-1 path for development, and the two differ only in the model library
selected at evaluator construction, so that retargeting to another kit reduces to
a netlist variant and a corner-library mapping.

Parallel throughput required one correction that dominates all others. ngspice
does not honour \code{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS} and instantiates an OpenMP pool per
process; with eight concurrent simulators, the spin-waiting threads oversubscribed
the twelve available cores by an order of magnitude, and eight concurrent
transients took \SI{90}{\second} each against \SI{1.5}{\second} in isolation.
Pinning each process to a single thread through a generated \code{.spiceinit}
restores \SI{1.4}{\second} for eight concurrent transients, a $65\times$
improvement, and is the difference between a training run of two hours and one of
several days. Environments are executed on threads rather than processes, since
the simulator runs in a subprocess that releases the interpreter lock.

\section{Reinforcement-Learning Formulation}
\label{sec:rl}

\subsection{Markov decision process}

\textbf{State.} The observation concatenates the normalised design vector, a
bias-validity flag, four scaled metrics (peaking, power, eye height, eye width),
and the normalised violation of every enforced constraint. Presenting violations
directly, rather than raw metrics alone, gives the critic a representation in
which distance from feasibility is explicit.

\textbf{Action.} Each action is an increment applied to the design vector, scaled
by $0.2$ of the action box and clipped to its bounds. Incremental actions let the
policy approach a target sizing through a short trajectory and, critically,
retreat from regions where the bias point collapses.

\textbf{Episode.} Up to 30 steps from an initial design drawn uniformly from the
action box. A bias failure is penalised but does not terminate the episode, so
the policy can recover from an invalid region rather than being reset out of it.

\textbf{Reward.} For normalised violations $v_i$, where $v_i=1$ denotes a miss of
one full specification's worth, and weights $w_i$:
\begin{equation}
r =
\begin{cases}
-\,\lambda_\mathrm{DC} & \text{bias invalid},\\[4pt]
\displaystyle -\sum_i w_i v_i \;-\; w_\mathrm{eff}\min\!\left(1,\frac{P}{P_\mathrm{max}}\right)
\;+\; \mathbb{1}[\,\forall i:\,v_i = 0\,]\Big(B + w_m \min(1, m)\Big) & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is supply power, $m$ is the tightest normalised constraint margin,
$B=20$ is the feasibility bonus, $w_m=5$ the margin weight, $w_\mathrm{eff}=1$
the continuous power charge, and $\lambda_\mathrm{DC}=10$ the bias penalty. The
power charge is applied whether or not the power constraint is satisfied, so the
reward retains a gradient after every constraint is met and the optimisation
continues to prefer the more efficient of two feasible designs.

\textbf{Algorithm.} Soft Actor-Critic with automatic entropy tuning from an
initial coefficient of $0.1$, batch size 256, one gradient step per environment
step, 500 random warm-up steps, and eight parallel environments. The
maximum-entropy objective suits a problem whose feasible set is a narrow,
irregularly shaped region: the entropy term maintains exploration along the
constraint boundary instead of collapsing onto the first feasible point
encountered.

\subsection{Reward design: margin versus marginal compliance}
\label{sec:reward-design}

The initial formulation terminated each episode at the first feasible design and
applied a bias penalty of $-100$. Training under it reached feasibility in
approximately 85\% of episodes but did not converge to a reliable policy and did
not outperform random search. Two independent causes were identified.

First, terminating on success removes any incentive to improve a feasible design.
The reward is maximised by reaching the feasible set, not by entering it, so the
converged policy sat exactly on the eye-height constraint boundary, where the
exploration noise inherent to a stochastic policy pushed a substantial fraction
of steps back outside it. Second, a bias penalty two orders of magnitude larger
than the unit-scale shaping terms dominated the critic's regression target,
devoting network capacity to modelling a discontinuity rather than the smooth
structure of the feasible region.

Three changes resolved both. Episodes continue after the first feasible step, so
feasible designs continue to accrue reward; an explicit margin bonus pays for the
tightest normalised slack, making a design with margin strictly preferable to one
without; and the bias penalty was reduced to $-10$, commensurate with the shaping
terms. Under the revised formulation the feasible-step fraction rose from 79\% to
95\% within 5k steps, and the median eye height moved from \SI{0.43}{\volt} to
\SI{0.92}{\volt} against a \SI{0.5}{\volt} target---from marginal compliance to
roughly $1.8\times$ the required opening. Deterministic rollouts reached
feasibility in 20 of 20 cases at a median of 2.5 steps. This distinction between
entering the feasible set and occupying its interior is, in our experience, the
single most consequential aspect of reward design for constrained analog sizing.

\subsection{Eye-height target calibration}
\label{sec:eye-target}

The eye targets were originally calibrated on the generic Level-1 model so that
approximately one design in ten drawn at random satisfies every constraint---a
feasibility density that keeps the reward informative without making the task
trivial. PSP103 devices deliver roughly half the gain of the idealised model, and
none of 400 random designs reaches \SI{0.5}{\volt} (best \SI{0.45}{\volt}, median
\SI{0.17}{\volt}). Transferring the calibration rather than the numeric target,
the requirement on the foundry models is set to \SI{0.25}{\volt}, which restores
the same 10\% random feasibility and remains comfortably above the
$\sim\SI{175}{\milli\volt}$ differential eye the link specification requires at
the receiver \cite{pcisig2007}, and well above the \SI{100}{\milli\volt}
acceptance threshold.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All results use the IHP PSP103 models with the eye-height requirement at
\SI{0.25}{\volt}, eight parallel environments, episodes held open after success,
random initial designs, margin weight 5, bias penalty $-10$, 30 steps per
episode, batch size 256, one gradient step per environment step, and automatic
entropy tuning from $0.1$. Training throughput is \SIrange{0.5}{0.7}{\second} per
environment step on a twelve-core laptop, so a 12k-step run completes in
approximately two hours.

Two quantities are reported throughout. \emph{Training feasibility} is the
fraction of environment steps in the final 4k steps at which every enforced
constraint is satisfied; it measures the reliability of the converged policy.
\emph{Steps to feasible} is obtained by rolling the policy out deterministically,
with exploration noise disabled, from 20 randomly drawn initial designs, and
counting simulations until the first fully feasible design; it measures sizing
cost for a new instance. The best design from each evaluation is then validated
against the complete gate set, the noise and area analyses, and all 45 corners.

\subsection{Ablation: selection on small-signal response alone}
\label{sec:ablation}

An informative ablation is the bounded search that preceded the
learning-based flow, which evaluates 100 candidates and selects on the AC gate
alone. Its chosen design is entirely compliant in the small-signal domain
(\SI{7.527}{\decibel} peaking, \SI{0.743}{\milli\watt}, HD3
\SI{-58.5}{\decibel}, noise \SI{0.242}{\milli\volt} rms, 45/45 corners) yet
delivers a post-channel eye of only \SI{0.160}{\volt} and \SI{0.58}{UI}
(Figure~\ref{fig:preml}) --- below the internal target and with substantially
less margin against the acceptance threshold than any design the policy
produces. A one-tap DFE swept over its bounds recovers \SI{0.310}{\volt}, but
cannot compensate for a stage that was never selected against the channel.

The ablation isolates the value of placing the transient eye inside the
optimisation loop rather than in post-hoc validation. Peaking and eye opening are
correlated but not interchangeable: a design can invert the channel's loss slope
at the Nyquist frequency and still fail to open the eye, because the eye depends
on the full transient response to the bit sequence, including group-delay
behaviour that a magnitude response does not capture. This is precisely the
observation made in the receiver literature \cite{naidu2022,saif2021}, and it is
the gap the framework closes.

\section{Corner Robustness and Design-Space Extensions}
\label{sec:curriculum}

\subsection{Corner-randomised curriculum}

Training at the nominal corner produces a policy that sizes correctly for TT,
\SI{1.2}{\volt}, \SI{27}{\celsius}, but nothing in that objective requires the
resulting designs to hold across process and temperature variation. Corner
robustness is therefore obtained by domain randomisation in a second stage: the
trained policy is resumed with each episode simulated at a corner drawn uniformly
from the 45-corner matrix, and the corner identity is deliberately withheld from
the observation. The policy cannot condition on the operating condition and must
instead infer the required sizing correction from the metrics it measures---which
is what a sizing procedure must do in practice, since the corner is not an
observable at design time.

The transfer is immediate and stable. Resumed from the HD3-enforced nominal
policy, the corner-randomised stage held 95--96\% feasible steps from its first
evaluation window, with per-process feasibility of SS 97\%, FS 96\%, FF 95\%,
TT 95\% and SF 94\%: the nominal policy transferred without a relearning phase,
and the near-uniform distribution across process corners indicates no systematic
weakness at the slow or fast extremes. Evaluated from random designs at randomly
drawn corners, 24 of 24 rollouts reached a fully feasible design over 19 distinct
corners spanning every process, supply and temperature setting, at a median of
two simulations and a worst case of four. The resulting design passes all 45
corners with HD3 at \SI{-61.9}{\decibel} and a peaking spread of approximately
\SI{0.6}{\decibel} across the matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:pvt}).

\subsection{Whole-equalizer sizing}
\label{sec:equalizer}

Extending the action space to six values places the DFE tap $b_1$ under the same
policy as the CTLE parameters, with the post-DFE eye height driving the reward.
This couples the two stages in the objective: the tap can compensate for residual
post-cursor interference, so the CTLE need not be sized as though it were the
only equalization in the path.

Trained for 8k steps, the six-value policy holds 91--93\% feasible steps with a
median post-DFE eye of \SI{0.51}{\volt}, against approximately \SI{0.35}{\volt}
for CTLE-only configurations. The tap is being spent on margin rather than on
reaching feasibility, which is the desired division of labour. Deterministic
rollouts reach feasibility in 20 of 20 cases at a median of three simulations;
the best design has a raw CTLE eye of \SI{0.325}{\volt} that a sized tap of
$+0.062$ opens to \SI{0.355}{\volt} with no bit errors over the PRBS7 sequence,
at \SI{1.36}{\milli\watt}, HD3 \SI{-71.6}{\decibel} and 45/45 corners
(Figure~\ref{fig:eyeeq}). The modest tap value is itself informative: a
well-sized CTLE leaves little post-cursor interference for the DFE to cancel, and
the optimisation converges on a balanced allocation rather than loading either
stage.

\subsection{Retargeting to a different Nyquist frequency}
\label{sec:tunable}

The specification requires peaking tunable over
\SIrange{1.25}{2.5}{\giga\hertz}. In this framework the Nyquist frequency is an
input rather than a constant: it sets the frequency at which peaking is measured,
the data rate and unit interval of the PRBS transient ($2\times$ Nyquist), the
transient time step, and the channel pole, so that the channel retains
approximately \SI{-10}{\decibel} of loss at the retargeted rate and the design
problem remains equivalently posed.

Tunability is demonstrated rather than asserted, because the requirement is not
automatically satisfied: the design sized at \SI{2.5}{\giga\hertz} peaks by only
\SI{2.87}{\decibel} at \SI{1.25}{\giga\hertz} and therefore fails the
\SI{3}{\decibel} floor at the lower rate. Resuming the seed-1 policy with the
specification set to \SI{1.25}{\giga\hertz} (PCIe Gen 1,
\SI{2.5}{\giga\bit\per\second}) recovers 93.5\% feasible steps within the first
1k steps---the policy transfers rather than relearning---and 96.5\% by 3k steps.
Evaluation reaches feasibility in 20 of 20 rollouts at a median of two
simulations, and the resulting design peaks \SI{5.23}{\decibel} at
\SI{1.25}{\giga\hertz} with a \SI{0.350}{\volt} / \SI{0.94}{UI} eye,
\SI{1.28}{\milli\watt}, HD3 \SI{-70.9}{\decibel} and 45/45 corners. The retuned
sizing moves the degeneration zero downward in frequency as circuit theory
requires ($R_s = \SI{365}{\ohm}$, $C_s = \SI{503}{\femto\farad}$, against
\SI{182}{\ohm} and \SI{522}{\femto\farad} at \SI{2.5}{\giga\hertz}), confirming
that the policy has learned the mechanism of peaking control rather than a single
memorised sizing.

\section{Natural-Language Reward Interface}
\label{sec:llm}

Reward weights encode engineering priorities, but expressing a priority as a
weight vector requires familiarity with the reward's internal scaling. The
framework therefore accepts design intent in plain language and maps it onto the
per-specification weights, the continuous power charge and the margin bonus. A
language model produces the mapping as structured JSON validated against a
schema; a keyword parser provides an offline fallback so the flow never depends
on service availability. The resulting settings are recorded in the run
configuration, keeping every trained policy traceable to the intent that shaped
it.

\begin{lstlisting}
> "We are power constrained: current budget matters much more than eye margin,
   and we don't care about linearity"
  weights: power 3.0, eye_vertical_v 0.33, hd3 0.33 (others 1.0)
  efficiency_weight 3.0; margin_weight 5.0

> "Open the eye as much as possible; power is not important"
  weights: eye_vertical_v 3.0, power 0.33; efficiency_weight 0.33
\end{lstlisting}

Weights are clipped to $[0.1, 10]$, so the interface reprioritises the objective
without allowing any constraint to be discarded entirely. Because sizing under a
re-weighted objective is exactly the amortisation case of
Section~\ref{sec:baselines}, a change of priorities costs two to three
simulations rather than a fresh optimisation.

\section{Limitations and Further Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Decision-feedback modelling.} The DFE in the optimisation loop is the
behavioural stage of Equation~\eqref{eq:dfe}, evaluated on sampled waveforms. A
clocked analog slicer with an explicit feedback delay is not yet simulated end to
end, so the tap is a signal-processing coefficient rather than a device-level
quantity. Simulating the slicer inside the transient gate would make the tap a
sized circuit parameter and is the highest-value extension.

\textbf{Area estimation.} Area is a first-order active-device geometry estimate
and is reported for compliance rather than offered as a layout result. The margin
against the ceiling is large enough that the conclusion is insensitive to the
estimate, but a post-layout figure would replace it.

\textbf{Corner observability.} The curriculum policy is robust because it is
trained blind to the operating corner, which is the correct formulation for
design-time sizing. Where the corner is known---in a diagnostic or
re-characterisation setting---including it in the observation would allow
corner-specific sizing.

\textbf{Design-space bounds.} $R_\mathrm{load}$ saturates at its
\SI{1}{\kilo\ohm} upper bound in most converged designs, indicating that the
optimum lies outside the box rather than within it. Widening the bound, and
admitting topology variants such as a second CTLE stage or inductive peaking,
is the most direct route to further margin.

\textbf{Objective revision during training.} Reward weights derived from design
intent take effect at the start of a run or resumed stage. Re-weighting an
in-progress run, and closing the loop so that each validation record informs the
next adjustment automatically, would make the language interface interactive
rather than configurational.
